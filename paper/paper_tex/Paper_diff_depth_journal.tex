\title{Split-Stem Active Depth Sensing for Slit Navigation}
\author{Anonymous Manuscript}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Differentiable simulation has made it possible to backpropagate task losses
through dynamics, planning, and control, but the perception stage is still
often treated as a non-differentiable boundary. As a result, gradient flow
stops at the sensor output and the agent cannot directly optimize how it
should sense the scene. We address this limitation with a differentiable
active depth perception framework for quadrotor slit navigation, where the
active depth sensor itself is modeled as part of the computational graph.
Projector power, exposure, and gain become trainable variables that shape the
observed depth map, allowing navigation loss to flow backward through sensing
and control to the sensor parameters. The resulting closed loop does not only
learn how to fly through a narrow aperture, but also how to configure the
camera to preserve task-relevant geometry under glare, specular reflection,
and low-reflectance degradation. To stabilize this learning process, we use a
split-stem policy that keeps camera semantics fixed during final flight
adaptation while still allowing the flight branch to improve. In held-out
closed-loop evaluation, the final policy achieves 0.751 success, 0.249
collision, 0.975 depth fill, and 0.550 terminal distance, improving over
fixed, random-fixed, non-differentiable learned-camera, and blind baselines.
These results show that active depth sensing can complete the differentiable
learning loop for embodied UAV navigation.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
active sensing, depth camera, slit navigation, DAgger, differentiable
perception, quadrotor navigation
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Differentiable robotics has recently expanded from dynamics and control into
full simulation pipelines. Differentiable simulators now provide gradients
through physical evolution, enabling robot policies to be trained with
analytical feedback rather than pure trial-and-error search
\cite{newburyReviewDifferentiableSimulators2024,zhangDiffAeroGPUAcceleratedDifferentiable2025,zhangLearningVisionbasedAgile2025,heegLearningQuadrotorControl2025a}. In parallel,
camera simulation has begun to support inverse rendering and embodied AI with
explicit optical modeling and calibration-aware rendering
\cite{chenDiffPhysCamDifferentiablePhysicsBased2025b}. These advances have
made the perception-to-control pipeline increasingly differentiable, but a key
gap remains: the sensing stage itself is still often optimized as a fixed
black box rather than as a trainable physical process.

This gap matters in vision-based UAV navigation, where the quality of a depth
observation directly depends on the sensor configuration. Projector power,
exposure, and gain are not merely image-style knobs. They determine whether the
geometry needed for obstacle avoidance is visible at all. The issue becomes
critical near narrow apertures, where glare, specular reflections, or weak
return can erase the local depth support needed by the controller. In such
cases, the policy may know that a maneuver failed, but it cannot propagate
that failure back to the sensing parameters that caused it.

Prior work has already shown that perception and control should not be
optimized independently. Perception-aware flight, trajectory planning, and
speed adaptation improve navigation by accounting for the sensing field of
view or the state-estimation regime
\cite{florenceIntegratedPerceptionControl2020,songLearningPerceptionAwareAgile2023,tordesillasDeepPANTHERLearningBasedPerceptionAware2023,zhaoLearningSpeedAdaptation2024a,huSeeingPixelMotion2025}. However,
these methods still rely on fixed sensing models, hand-designed sensing rules,
or teacher-student distillation. They do not provide a differentiable path
from task loss to the physical parameters of the sensor itself.

In this paper, we close that loop with a differentiable active depth
perception framework for slit navigation. The key idea is to model the active
depth sensor as a differentiable module inside the sim-to-real training
pipeline, so that the navigation loss can flow backward through sensing,
simulation, and control to the sensor parameters. This enables the agent to
learn not only how to fly through a narrow aperture, but also how to configure
the camera so that the aperture geometry remains observable under degraded
visual conditions. To keep this end-to-end learning stable, we further use a
split-stem policy and a DAgger-style relabeling stage that separate camera
semantics from final flight adaptation \cite{ross2011dagger}.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a differentiable active depth perception model that makes
    projector power, exposure, and gain part of the computational graph.
    \item We integrate that model into a closed-loop UAV learning pipeline so
    that task gradients can propagate through perception, simulation, and
    control.
    \item We introduce a split-stem adaptation strategy and teacher relabeling
    procedure to stabilize the learned camera semantics during flight
    optimization.
    \item We validate the framework on a slit-navigation benchmark with glare,
    specular, and low-reflectance depth degradation regimes, showing improved
    closed-loop performance over fixed, random-fixed, non-differentiable, and
    blind baselines.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Differentiable Simulation and Control}
Differentiable simulation has become a core mechanism for closing the gap
between physical dynamics and gradient-based policy learning
\cite{newburyReviewDifferentiableSimulators2024}. In aerial robotics, this has
enabled policies to be optimized directly through physics models, from
diffusion-like differentiable control pipelines to vision-based agile flight
\cite{zhangDiffAeroGPUAcceleratedDifferentiable2025,zhangLearningVisionbasedAgile2025,heegLearningQuadrotorControl2025a,romeroActorCriticModelPredictive2025}.
These studies establish the key premise that task loss can be propagated
through dynamics and control, so that the learned policy is not tuned by
heuristic reward shaping alone. However, their differentiability usually ends
at the observation interface: the simulator can backpropagate through motion,
but the sensing process is still treated as an input generator rather than as a
trainable physical module.

\subsection{Differentiable Rendering and Camera Simulation}
The second line of work concerns photorealistic simulation and inverse
rendering. Procedural renderers and robotic simulators such as BlenderProc2,
VISTA 2.0, Habitat 3.0, and Chrono::Sensor have made it easier to synthesize
large-scale visual data for embodied AI
\cite{denningerBlenderProc22023,aminiVISTA2022,puigHabitat32023,elmquistSensorSimulation2021}.
In parallel, camera-simulation studies have examined how sensor parameters,
optical artifacts, and calibration affect downstream perception
\cite{blasinskiOptimizingImage2018,liuCameraParameters2020,lyuValidationImageSystems2022,carlsonModelingCameraEffects2018,carlsonSensorTransfer2019}.
DiffPhysCam extends this direction by making the camera itself differentiable
and physically calibrated, so that optical effects such as defocus blur,
exposure variation, and other sensor artifacts can be optimized together with
the rest of the pipeline \cite{chenDiffPhysCamDifferentiablePhysicsBased2025b}.

Recent differentiable-rendering systems further improve the fidelity of
simulation-based learning. NeRF, Instant-NGP, Nerfstudio, Gaussian Splatting,
SuGaR, and NVDiffRecMC have all advanced the reconstruction and synthesis of
3D scenes from images \cite{mildenhallNeRF2020,mullerInstantNGP2022,tancikNerfstudio2023,kerblGaussianSplatting2023,guedonSuGaR2024,hasselgrenNVDiffRecMC2022}.
At the same time, several works model camera optics explicitly within the
rendering loop, including Neural Camera Simulators, depth-of-field-aware
differentiable rendering, and NeRF variants with editable camera settings or
thin-lens effects \cite{ouyangNeuralCameraSimulators2021,pidhorskyiDepthOfField2022,sunCaSENeRF2023,kimLensNeRF2024,wuDoFNeRF2022,wangNeRFocus2022}.
These methods are highly relevant to our setting, but they mainly target
photorealism or inverse rendering. By contrast, our objective is task-driven
active sensing: the camera settings are optimized because they change the
navigation outcome, not only because they improve image realism.

\subsection{Perception-Aware and Active UAV Navigation}
Vision-based UAV navigation has long recognized that perception and control
must be coupled. Classical active-perception work established sensing as an
action that should be chosen to improve future decisions
\cite{bajcsy1988active,aloimonos1988active,revisitingactiveperception}.
Modern UAV systems follow the same principle in more task-specific forms,
including integrated perception-control, perception-aware agile flight,
perception-aware trajectory planning, and speed adaptation in clutter
\cite{florenceIntegratedPerceptionControl2020,songLearningPerceptionAwareAgile2023,tordesillasDeepPANTHERLearningBasedPerceptionAware2023,zhaoLearningSpeedAdaptation2024a,huSeeingPixelMotion2025}.
Agile-flight platforms and benchmark systems further show how tightly coupled
perception-action design improves high-speed flight
\cite{loquercioDeepDroneRacing2018,loquercioLearningHighSpeed2021,foehnAgilicious2022,kaufmannChampionLevelDroneRacing2023}.
These works motivate the present paper, but they still assume a fixed sensing
model or a fixed observation encoder. Our contribution is to make the sensor
parameters themselves differentiable and to use that gradient signal to close
the loop between flight, sensing, and task loss.

\subsection{Imitation Learning and Closed-Loop Relabeling}
The training procedure is also related to imitation learning under covariate
shift. DAgger reduces compounding error by relabeling the states encountered by
the learner rather than restricting supervision to expert trajectories
\cite{ross2011dagger}. We reuse that idea at the sensor level: a differentiable
camera teacher generates relabeling targets for the states visited by the
current policy, and the learned camera branch is then stabilized before flight
adaptation. This separation is important because camera semantics and flight
control are coupled but not identical objectives. The relabeling stage keeps
the perception branch interpretable while the final flight-only optimization
consumes the resulting observation distribution.

\section{Method Overview}
\noindent
Let $\mathbf{x}_t$ be the quadrotor state, $\mathbf{c}_t=[P_t,E_t,G_t]^\top$
the active-depth camera state, and $\rho_t\in\{\mathrm{glare},\mathrm{specular},
\mathrm{dark}\}$ the local sensing regime. The proposed method learns a
closed-loop map
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_\theta:
    (D_t^{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{c}_t,\mathbf{h}_t,\mathbf{q}_t)
    \mapsto
    (\mathbf{u}_t,\mathbf{c}_{t+1}^{\star},\mathbf{h}_{t+1},\mathbf{q}_{t+1}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u}_t$ is a local acceleration command and
$\mathbf{c}_{t+1}^{\star}$ is an absolute camera target. The main technical
choice is to represent the sensor as a differentiable module
$\mathcal{S}_{\phi}$ and to train the camera branch through teacher
optimization and online relabeling, instead of relying on sparse collision
gradients alone.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Differentiable System}
The rollout recurrence is
\begin{align}
    D_t^{\mathrm{raw}} &=
    \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathcal{M}), \label{eq:raw_depth}\\
    (D_t^{\mathrm{obs}},Q_t,M_t) &=
    \mathcal{S}_{\phi}(D_t^{\mathrm{raw}},\mathbf{c}_t,\rho_t,\mathbf{x}_t),
    \label{eq:sensor}\\
    \mathbf{s}_t &=
    \psi_{\mathrm{flight}}(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{goal}},
    \mathbf{c}_t), \label{eq:state}\\
    \mathbf{m}_t &=
    \psi_{\mathrm{cam}}(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{c}_t), \label{eq:cam_motion}\\
    (\mathbf{u}_t,\mathbf{c}_{t+1}^{\star},\mathbf{h}_{t+1},\mathbf{q}_{t+1})
    &= \Pi_\theta(D_t^{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{c}_t,
    \mathbf{m}_t,\mathbf{h}_t,\mathbf{q}_t), \label{eq:policy}\\
    \mathbf{c}_{t+1}
    &= \alpha\mathbf{c}_t+(1-\alpha)\mathbf{c}_{t+1}^{\star},
    \quad \alpha=0.7, \label{eq:camera_ema}\\
    \mathbf{x}_{t+1}
    &= f_{\mathrm{dyn}}(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{u}_t,\Delta t_t).
    \label{eq:dynamics}
\end{align}
Here $\mathcal{R}$ is the geometric renderer and $\mathcal{M}$ is the slit
scene. The state encoder $\psi_{\mathrm{flight}}$ includes goal-relative local
motion and safety margin; $\psi_{\mathrm{cam}}$ includes current camera state,
local velocity, and attitude but excludes the goal vector. This prevents the
camera controller from using a privileged geometric schedule.

For a horizon $T$, the differentiable training objective can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J}(\theta)=
    \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
    \ell_{\mathrm{nav}}(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{u}_t)
    +\ell_{\mathrm{sens}}(M_t,\mathbf{c}_t,\mathbf{x}_t).
    \label{eq:full_objective}
\end{equation}
The camera receives task information only if
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{J}}{\partial \mathbf{c}_t}
    =
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{J}}{\partial D_t^{\mathrm{obs}}}
    \frac{\partial D_t^{\mathrm{obs}}}{\partial \mathbf{c}_t}
    +
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{J}}{\partial \mathbf{x}_{t+1}}
    \frac{\partial \mathbf{x}_{t+1}}{\partial \mathbf{u}_t}
    \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}_t}{\partial D_t^{\mathrm{obs}}}
    \frac{\partial D_t^{\mathrm{obs}}}{\partial \mathbf{c}_t}
    \label{eq:camera_gradient}
\end{equation}
is nonzero. Equation~\eqref{eq:camera_gradient} is the motivation for making
active perception differentiable.

\subsection{Differentiable Active-Depth Model}
The sensor maps camera registers to a soft validity field. Exposure and gain
are
\begin{align}
    \tau(E_t) &=
    \mathrm{clip}(t_{\min}+t_{\mathrm{span}}E_t,t_{\mathrm{lo}},t_{\mathrm{hi}}),
    \label{eq:exp_time}\\
    \Gamma(G_t) &=
    \Gamma_0+\Gamma_s
    \frac{(G_t+\epsilon)^\gamma-\epsilon^\gamma}
    {(1+\epsilon)^\gamma-\epsilon^\gamma}.
    \label{eq:iso_gain}
\end{align}
Given $Z=D_t^{\mathrm{raw}}$, the active and passive returns are
\begin{align}
    S_{\mathrm{act}}(Z,\mathbf{c}_t)
    &=\frac{k_aP_t\tau(E_t)}{Z^2+c_d}, \\
    S_{\mathrm{pass}}(\mathbf{c}_t)
    &=k_p\tau(E_t)\sqrt{\Gamma(G_t)}.
\end{align}
Let $B_t$ denote range, edge, ambient, blur, and noise penalties, and let
$A_{\rho_t}$ denote the regime-specific artifact map. The differentiable
quality and validity maps are
\begin{align}
    Q_t &=
    \mathrm{clip}\Bigl(
    \sigma\bigl(a_s(S_{\mathrm{act}}+S_{\mathrm{pass}})-B_t\bigr)
    -A_{\rho_t},0,1\Bigr), \label{eq:quality}\\
    M_t &= \sigma\left(\frac{Q_t-\tau_q}{\sigma_q}\right), \label{eq:validity}\\
    D_t^{\mathrm{obs}} &=
    Z\odot\left(\mathrm{sg}(\mathbb{I}[M_t>0.5]-M_t)+M_t\right).
    \label{eq:st_depth}
\end{align}
The straight-through term in \eqref{eq:st_depth} gives hard invalid pixels in
the forward pass and soft gradients in the backward pass.

The regime artifact $A_{\rho_t}$ is localized around the slit:
\begin{equation}
    A_{\rho_t}=
    \begin{cases}
    A_{\mathrm{glare}}(E_t,G_t,P_t), & \rho_t=\mathrm{glare},\\
    A_{\mathrm{spec}}(P_t,E_t,G_t), & \rho_t=\mathrm{specular},\\
    A_{\mathrm{dark}}(P_t,E_t,G_t), & \rho_t=\mathrm{dark}.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:regimes}
\end{equation}
Glare penalizes high exposure/gain near the aperture, specular material
penalizes high active return near side-wall patches, and dark material rewards
exposure/gain recovery while retaining blur/noise costs.

\subsection{Split-Stem Policy}
The observed depth is encoded by two complementary channels:
\begin{align}
    \phi_t^{\mathrm{near}} &=
    \mathrm{Pool}_{\max}
    \left(\mathcal{N}(1/\tilde D_t)\odot M_t\right),\\
    \phi_t^{\mathrm{far}} &=
    \mathrm{Pool}_{\mathrm{avg}}
    \left(\mathcal{N}(\tilde D_t)\odot M_t\right),\\
    \Phi_t &= [\phi_t^{\mathrm{near}},\phi_t^{\mathrm{far}}].
    \label{eq:depth_encoding}
\end{align}
The flight and camera branches use separate visual stems:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{z}_t^f &= F_f(\Phi_t),&
    \mathbf{z}_t^c &= F_c(\Phi_t),\\
    \boldsymbol{\xi}_t &= [\mathbf{z}_t^f,W_s\mathbf{s}_t],&
    \mathbf{g}_t &= \sigma(W_g\boldsymbol{\xi}_t),\\
    \mathbf{r}_t &=
    \varphi\!\left(\mathbf{g}_t\odot\mathbf{z}_t^f+
    (1-\mathbf{g}_t)\odot W_s\mathbf{s}_t\right),\\
    \mathbf{h}_{t+1} &= \mathrm{GRU}_f(\mathbf{r}_t,\mathbf{h}_t),&
    \mathbf{u}_t &= W_u\varphi(\mathbf{h}_{t+1}),\\
    \mathbf{a}_t^c &=
    [A_c(\mathbf{z}_t^c),W_c\mathbf{c}_t,W_m\mathbf{m}_t],\\
    \mathbf{q}_{t+1} &= \mathrm{GRU}_c(\mathbf{a}_t^c,\mathbf{q}_t),\\
    \mathbf{c}_{t+1}^{\star} &= \sigma(W_o\varphi(\mathbf{q}_{t+1})).
    \label{eq:split_stem}
\end{align}
The camera stem $F_c$ is initialized from $F_f$ but has independent parameters.
During flight-only adaptation, all camera-side parameters are frozen:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \Theta_{\mathrm{cam}}=\{&
    F_c,A_c,W_c,W_m,\mathrm{GRU}_c,W_o,\\
    &\mathrm{camera\ normalization\ layers}\}.
    \label{eq:freeze_set}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Teacher Optimization}
For each visited state, the camera teacher solves a local differentiable
optimization problem. Let $\bar{\mathbf{c}}=\sigma(\boldsymbol{\eta})$ be the
teacher candidate. Define patch fill as a lower-tail average over pooled
validity:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{p}(\bar{\mathbf{c}})
    &=\mathrm{vec}\!\left(
    \mathrm{Pool}_{6\times8}(M(\bar{\mathbf{c}}))\right),\\
    r_{\mathrm{fill}}(\bar{\mathbf{c}})
    &=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i\in\mathrm{BottomK}(\mathbf{p},K)}p_i .
    \label{eq:fill_cvar}
\end{align}
The teacher objective is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \bar{\mathbf{c}}_{t+1}
    &=\arg\min_{\bar{\mathbf{c}}\in[0,1]^3}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{teach}}(\bar{\mathbf{c}}),\\
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{teach}}
    =&\lambda_f[r_{\min}-r_{\mathrm{fill}}(\bar{\mathbf{c}})]_+^2
    +\lambda_b\|v_t\tau(\bar E)\|_2^2\\
    &+\lambda_n\bar G^2
    +\lambda_s\|\bar{\mathbf{c}}-\mathbf{c}_t\|_2^2\\
    &+\lambda_m\omega_t
    \|\bar{\mathbf{c}}-\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{nom}}\|_2^2 .
    \label{eq:teacher_loss}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In the final DAgger relabeling stage, \eqref{eq:teacher_loss} is optimized for
120 Adam steps with learning rate $0.08$; teacher camera EMA is disabled.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Camera Teacher Dataset and Head Pretraining}
\label{alg:teacher_pretrain}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require frozen policy $\Pi_{\theta_0}$, scenarios $\mathcal{R}$,
rollouts $N$, horizon $T$
\Ensure camera-pretrained checkpoint $\theta_{\mathrm{cam}}$
\State $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cam}}\gets\emptyset$
\For{$\rho\in\mathcal{R}$}
  \For{$n=1,\ldots,N$}
    \State reset simulator with regime $\rho$
    \State initialize $\mathbf{c}_0=[0.5,0.5,0.5]^\top$
    \For{$t=0,\ldots,T-1$}
      \State render $D_t^{\mathrm{obs}}$ using \eqref{eq:sensor}
      \State compute $\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{m}_t$ by
      \eqref{eq:state}--\eqref{eq:cam_motion}
      \State $(\mathbf{u}_t,\mathbf{c}_{t+1}^{\star})\gets
      \Pi_{\theta_0}(D_t^{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{c}_t,\mathbf{m}_t)$
      \State $\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{t+1}\gets
      \arg\min \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{teach}}$ by \eqref{eq:teacher_loss}
      \State append
      $(D_t^{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{c}_t,\mathbf{m}_t,
      \bar{\mathbf{c}}_{t+1},\rho)$ to $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cam}}$
      \State step dynamics with $\mathbf{u}_t$
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State freeze $\Theta\setminus\Theta_{\mathrm{cam}}$
\State train $\Theta_{\mathrm{cam}}$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cam}}$ by
\eqref{eq:supervised_loss}
\State \Return best validation checkpoint $\theta_{\mathrm{cam}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Camera Supervision and DAgger Relabeling}
For a sequence dataset
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{cam}}=
    \{D^{\mathrm{obs}},\mathbf{s},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{m},
    \bar{\mathbf{c}},\rho,x_{\mathrm{local}},r_{\mathrm{fill}}\},
    \label{eq:dataset}
\end{equation}
the camera branch is trained by recurrent supervised regression:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}
    =&\frac{1}{BT}\sum_{b,t}
    \mathrm{SmoothL1}
    (\mathbf{c}_{b,t+1}^{\star},\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{b,t+1})\\
    &+\lambda_{\Delta c}
    \|\mathbf{c}_{b,t+1}^{\star}-\mathbf{c}_{b,t}^{\star}\|_2^2 .
    \label{eq:supervised_loss}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The final relabeled dataset contains 144 sequences and 11,520 labels, with
teacher mean/std $P/E/G=0.584/0.408/0.403$ and $0.164/0.197/0.181$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{DAgger Relabeling and Flight-Only Adaptation}
\label{alg:dagger_flightonly}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require offline camera checkpoint $\theta_{\mathrm{pre}}$,
teacher loss \eqref{eq:teacher_loss}
\Ensure final split-stem policy $\theta^\star$
\State $\theta\gets\theta_{\mathrm{pre}}$
\State $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{dagger}}\gets\emptyset$
\For{online learned-camera rollout}
  \For{$t=0,\ldots,T-1$}
    \State student predicts $\mathbf{c}_{t+1}^{\star}$ and updates
    $\mathbf{c}_{t+1}$ by \eqref{eq:camera_ema}
    \State teacher labels the visited state:
    $\bar{\mathbf{c}}_{t+1}\gets\arg\min \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{teach}}$
    \State append online sample to $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{dagger}}$
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State retrain $\Theta_{\mathrm{cam}}$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{dagger}}$
using \eqref{eq:supervised_loss}
\State freeze $\Theta_{\mathrm{cam}}$ and detach
$(\mathbf{c}_{t+1}^{\star},\mathbf{q}_{t+1})$
\State optimize $\Theta\setminus\Theta_{\mathrm{cam}}$ by
\eqref{eq:flight_loss} under learned-camera rollouts
\State \Return $\theta^\star$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Flight Adaptation Objective}
The final flight-only stage optimizes
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{flight}}
    ={}&\lambda_v\mathcal{L}_v
    +\lambda_a\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{acc}}
    +\lambda_j\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{jerk}}
    +\lambda_c\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{collide}},\\
    \theta^\star
    ={}&\arg\min_{\theta\setminus\Theta_{\mathrm{cam}}}
    \mathbb{E}_{\rho,\mathbf{x}_0}
    \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{flight}}(t),
    \quad \Theta_{\mathrm{cam}}\ \mathrm{frozen}.
    \label{eq:flight_loss}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Thus, the camera branch defines the observation distribution, while the flight
branch alone is updated to exploit that distribution.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Evaluation Rationale}
The experiments are organized around the central differentiability claim. If
the proposed sensor model really closes the perception-simulation-control
loop, then three outcomes should appear together. First, active camera control
should improve closed-loop navigation compared with fixed or task-agnostic
camera settings. Second, the improvement should be accompanied by healthier
depth observations, because the mechanism is improved sensing rather than
arbitrary flight regularization. Third, the camera response should be
scene-specific near the slit: glare should suppress exposure and gain, whereas
low-reflectance material should increase them.

This rationale determines the organization of the evaluation. Overall success
and collision quantify the task-level endpoint of the gradient chain. Depth
fill measures whether the observation stream remains usable. Phase-wise camera
statistics and matched-pose depth sequences test whether the learned behavior
is consistent with the intended active-sensing mechanism rather than a
trajectory artifact.

\subsection{Benchmark Configuration}
Each episode lasts 60 control steps at 15 Hz, so the closed-loop horizon spans
four seconds. The raw depth renderer produces a $96 \times 72$ image, and the
policy receives a $48 \times 36$ encoded depth input. The task uses the same
wall, slit, start, and goal geometry across all sensing regimes. The slit
center is sampled laterally, and the entire scene can be randomly rotated in
yaw. This rotation means that success requires using the local depth and state
cues rather than a fixed world-frame direction.

The camera action is three-dimensional and normalized to $[0,1]^3$. The fixed
baseline uses the nominal setting. The random-fixed baseline samples a static
camera setting for each episode and keeps it fixed. Learned-camera methods
update the camera state through the smoothed camera command described above.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Reporting}
An episode is successful when the vehicle reaches the goal region without
collision. Collision is recorded if the trajectory intersects the wall or
violates the clearance proxy. We also report terminal distance to the goal and
the mean valid-depth fill rate. Let $\mathbf{x}_t$ denote the drone position,
$F_t$ the fraction of valid depth pixels at time $t$, and $\rho_t$ the minimum
clearance to any obstacle. We summarize
\begin{align}
\mathrm{success} &= \mathbb{1}[\|\mathbf{x}_T-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{goal}}\|_2 \le \delta
\ \wedge\ \neg \mathrm{collision}], \\
\mathrm{collision} &= \mathbb{1}[\exists t:\ \rho_t \le \epsilon], \\
\mathrm{fill} &= \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} F_t, \\
d_{\mathrm{final}} &= \|\mathbf{x}_T-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{goal}}\|_2 .
\end{align}
Binary outcomes are reported with Wilson 95\% confidence intervals. Continuous
episode metrics, including fill and terminal distance, are reported with
bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals.

Camera behavior is summarized within three local phases relative to the wall,
where $x_t$ denotes the scalar local-forward component of $\mathbf{x}_t$ after
transforming the position into the wall frame:
\begin{equation}
x_t \in
\begin{cases}
\text{before}, & x_t < -0.25~\mathrm{m},\\
\text{near}, & |x_t| \le 0.25~\mathrm{m},\\
\text{after}, & x_t > 0.25~\mathrm{m}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The near phase is the most important because the depth image then determines
whether the vehicle can align with the slit and avoid the wall. We report
episode-aggregated near-slit power, exposure, and gain, as well as the
glare-dark camera separation measured by the mean absolute parameter
difference. These phase-wise statistics are diagnostic rather than training
targets.

\section{Results}
\noindent
The results are presented as evidence for a closed-loop sensing mechanism. We
first check that training converges, then test whether differentiable active
sensing improves navigation, and finally inspect whether the learned sensor
actions are physically interpretable near the aperture.

\subsection{Training Curves Are a Convergence Check, Not the Main Evidence}
Figure~\ref{fig:train_curve} reports training loss, success, and collision
curves for the comparison methods. Their role is diagnostic rather than
decisive: they show that the policies reached stable regimes and that the final
flight-only run is not an incomplete optimization artifact. Final claims use
held-out closed-loop evaluation with the same episode budget and scene
distribution for every method.

For the final split-stem run, the training summary and held-out evaluation are
consistent. The training summary reports approximately 0.750 success and 0.250
collision, while held-out evaluation gives 0.751 success and 0.249 collision.
This agreement is notable because an earlier shared-stem variant produced a
larger mismatch: the camera branch appeared useful during training, but its
semantics were not stable under evaluation. The split-stem layout removes that
failure mode by fixing the camera features during flight-only adaptation. The
training curve therefore acts as a consistency check for the final system,
not as a substitute for held-out evaluation.

\subsection{Active Camera Control Improves Closed-Loop Navigation}
The primary evaluation is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:journal_main_navigation}
and Figure~\ref{fig:navigation}. The proposed policy succeeds in 0.751 of
episodes, compared with 0.667 for fixed camera, 0.627 for random fixed camera,
0.580 for the non-differentiable learned-camera baseline, and 0.032 for the
blind controller. The collision rate falls from 0.333 for fixed camera to 0.249
for the proposed policy. The improvement is not only a geometric-control
effect: depth fill rises from 0.721 under fixed camera to 0.975 under the
active policy.

confidence intervals. Success and collision are episode-level rates with
Wilson intervals, fill is the mean valid-depth fraction with bootstrap
intervals, and $\Delta$ columns are measured relative to the fixed-camera
baseline.

The joint improvement in success and fill is the key point. A gain in success
without a gain in fill could be explained by a more conservative policy or a
different trajectory distribution. A gain in fill without a gain in success
would mean that the camera learned to recover pixels that the controller cannot
use. The proposed method is the only learned-camera variant that improves both
metrics over the fixed baseline. Random-fixed and non-differentiable baselines
do increase fill, but they do not improve success. That separation matters:
the recovered pixels must be scene-conditioned and must appear at the right
part of the aperture to affect collision avoidance. The final policy therefore
improves the sensing stream and then converts that improved observation into
higher success.

\subsection{Scene-Level Results Show Where the Gain Occurs}
The scene breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:journal_scene_breakdown} reveals that the
benefit is not uniform across regimes. Dark material benefits the most because
it is fundamentally a return-starvation problem: additional exposure and gain
can recover weak returns without erasing the slit boundary, so the extra fill
translates cleanly into more successful traversals. Specular scenes are
intermediate because the dominant failure mode is local reflection and edge
instability around the side-wall patches; the learned camera can damp that
instability, but it cannot eliminate it. Glare is the hardest regime because it
is a saturation problem near the aperture boundary. The camera can suppress
overexposure and recover a large amount of valid depth, yet the remaining error
is concentrated exactly where the controller needs clean contrast, so the gain
in success is smaller than the gain in fill.
Each scene-level entry in Table~\ref{tab:journal_scene_breakdown} is reported
as success/fill, where fill is the mean valid-depth fraction.

This asymmetry is the mechanism-level result. Dark and glare both gain fill,
but only dark converts most of that gain into success. The difference is
physical rather than statistical: dark suppresses return that can be recovered
by integration and gain, whereas glare corrupts the contrast at the aperture
itself. Fill is therefore necessary but not sufficient; the controller also
needs the recovered pixels to preserve the slit boundary.

\subsection{The Learned Camera Implements Scene-Specific Near-Slit Behavior}
Table~\ref{tab:journal_camera_response} and Figure~\ref{fig:camera_mechanism}
test whether the learned camera has interpretable active-sensing semantics. In
the near-slit phase, the proposed policy uses high power but low exposure and
gain in glare, high exposure and gain in dark material, and a lower-power
intermediate setting in specular scenes. The direction of change is physically
coherent. In glare, suppressing exposure and gain protects the aperture
boundary from saturation. In dark material, increasing exposure and gain
compensates for weak return. Specular scenes sit between those extremes, with
lower power to avoid reflective hotspots and moderate exposure and gain to
stabilize the adjacent patches. The glare-dark separation reaches 0.412 with a
narrow confidence interval, whereas fixed camera, non-differentiable learned
camera, and blind control remain at or near zero. Random fixed camera has small
separation because its sampled settings differ between episodes, but that
separation is not scene-conditional and remains far below the proposed policy.
Table~\ref{tab:journal_camera_response} reports normalized near-slit camera
commands as $P/E/G$, corresponding to projector power, exposure, and gain.
The G--D L1 column is the mean absolute difference between glare and dark
near-slit camera commands, with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals.

\subsection{Camera Relabeling and Flight Adaptation Are Complementary}
Figure~\ref{fig:dagger_diag} separates two questions that are easy to conflate.
The first is whether the camera branch has learned meaningful scene-dependent
semantics. The second is whether the flight branch can use the resulting
observation distribution to navigate. Camera pretraining and DAgger relabeling
answer the first question. They recover clear glare-dark camera separation in
online rollouts, with high exposure and gain in dark material and low exposure
and gain in glare. However, these camera-only policies do not answer the
second question: they produce meaningful camera semantics, but the flight
controller has not yet adapted to the resulting observation stream.

This is expected. A learned camera changes the distribution of depth images
seen by the controller. Even if the camera is semantically correct, the flight
policy still has to adapt to the new observation stream. The final flight-only
stage performs that adaptation while freezing the camera branch. The result is
that the final policy preserves the DAgger-learned camera separation while
achieving substantially higher navigation success than the camera-only
diagnostic policies. The diagnostic checkpoints are therefore useful rather
than contradictory: they show that camera semantics can be learned before they
become operationally useful for closed-loop flight.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What the Evidence Supports}
The experiments support a focused claim: in this controlled slit-navigation
benchmark, task-aware active depth sensing improves closed-loop flight when the
camera semantics learned from a differentiable teacher are preserved during
final policy adaptation. The evidence is not based on a single metric. Success
improves relative to the primary baselines, collision decreases, depth fill
increases substantially, near-slit camera settings separate according to scene
regime, and matched-pose renderings show that those settings change the
observed depth image under the same geometry.

The regime asymmetry is the most informative part of that result. Dark scenes
improve the most in success because they are return-starvation problems:
additional exposure and gain recover weak returns without destroying the slit
boundary. Glare improves more in fill than in success because it is a
saturation problem: the camera can recover valid pixels, but the aperture edge
is still vulnerable to contamination. Specular scenes sit between those
extremes because local reflection creates holes and edge drift that the camera
can mitigate but not completely remove. The benchmark therefore shows not just
that active sensing helps, but that it helps for physically meaningful reasons.

The split-stem design is important for this conclusion. The camera branch is
not merely another head attached to a representation that continues to move
during flight training. It has its own copied visual pathway and camera-specific
modules, and these components are frozen during flight-only adaptation. This
keeps the meaning of the camera head stable while allowing the flight branch to
adapt to the learned-camera depth stream.

\subsection{What the Evidence Does Not Claim}
The result should not be read as a claim that active camera control solves
general aerial navigation or that depth fill alone determines success. The
benchmark is intentionally narrow, and the strongest navigation gain occurs in
the dark-material regime. Glare remains difficult: the active camera improves
fill substantially, but success improves only modestly. This distinction is
important. A valid depth pixel is useful only if it preserves the task-critical
aperture cue at the right time and location. Global fill is therefore a
necessary but incomplete indicator of navigation quality. The random-fixed and
non-differentiable baselines make the same point from the negative side: they
can raise fill, but if the recovered pixels are not scene-conditioned, the
navigation policy does not improve.

Nor should the pretrain and DAgger diagnostic policies be interpreted as failed
camera controllers. Their purpose is to show that camera semantics can be
learned and preserved. Their low navigation success demonstrates a different
point: the flight controller must be adapted after the camera changes the
closed-loop observation distribution.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
The study is simulation-only. The sensor model is designed to preserve the
causal relationships among power, exposure, gain, motion blur, noise, and
localized depth degradation, but it is not a pixel-accurate model of any
specific commercial depth camera. Transferring the method to hardware would
require calibration of the sensor parameters, validation under real lighting
and material conditions, and safeguards for register limits and temporal camera
latency.

The evaluation also uses a single training seed. The held-out episode count is
large enough to estimate closed-loop performance for the trained checkpoints,
but it does not measure variance across independently trained policies. A
stronger future study would report multiple training seeds, additional slit
widths, and hardware-in-the-loop or real-robot trials. These extensions are
outside the scope of the present benchmark, whose purpose is to isolate the
mechanism of active depth sensing under controlled degradation.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a split-stem active depth sensing pipeline for quadrotor
slit navigation. The method treats camera parameters as part of the closed
loop, learns camera behavior from differentiable teacher relabeling, and then
adapts the flight branch while freezing the camera branch. In held-out
evaluation, the final policy improves success and depth fill over fixed,
random-fixed, non-differentiable learned-camera, and blind baselines. The
mechanism analysis shows that this improvement is accompanied by interpretable
scene-specific camera behavior near the slit. These results indicate that
active depth control is most effective when sensing semantics and flight
adaptation are treated as coupled but separately stabilized parts of the policy.
